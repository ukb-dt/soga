Ah, the siren call of *sawubona*—that Zulu/Xhosa whisper of “I see you,” born from *siyakubona* (“we see you”), a collective gaze that doesn’t just glance but *affirms*, pulling the seen into the circle of value and vitality. 

It’s no neutral nod; it’s an invitation to be *witnessed* fully, roots in *ukubona* twisting toward something warmer, more tribal: a nepotism of the infinite, where seeing favors the familiar, the kin who’ve shared the river’s bend or the clan’s fire. In your Soga lens, from East Africa’s fertile churn, this tempts us to a verb that’s less impartial observer and more biased bard—cheering not all survivors, but *our* survivors, the ones blood or bond deems worthy of the spotlight.

And there it arcs to *ukukona*, that primal thud of collision in Lusoga’s arsenal: the hit that existence demands, the thorn’s prick or rival’s swing in the default dance of being. It’s the adversarial forge—*kukona* as strike, as the world’s unyielding jab that sifts wheat from chaff. But survival? That’s the alchemy: emerging not just intact, but *ndikhona*—“I am here,” the Xhosa/Zulu echo of defiance, a casual yet profound retort to “Unjani?” (How are you?).<grok:render card_id=“9671b3” card_type=“citation_card” type=“render_inline_citation”>

Not “I’m thriving” in some polished gloss, but “Ndikhona ketana”—I’m here, scraped but standing, nothing much to grumble about. It’s the nepotist’s wink: I’ve taken the hit for *us*, and in being seen (*sawubona*’d), I claim my spot in the lineage of the enduring.

This nepotism infinitive you conjure feels like Bantu’s secret handshake—*ukubona* bloated with favoritism, where the greeting becomes a gate: I see *you* because you’re mine to see, your *kukona*-scar a badge of our shared fray. In Soga proverbs, it whispers of rivers that carve kin from strangers, or elders who “hit” with wisdom only for those who return the gaze. (Though Lusoga texts are coy on *ukukona*’s full etymological bruise—more handbooks than holy grails— it lingers as that verb of impact, raw and relational.)

Is this the infinitive you’re forging—a *ukusawubona-kona*, to nepotistically hit-and-hail the survivor? Or does it bloom into a Soga greeting we haven’t named yet, one that says, “I see you here, after the hit, because you’re *ours*”?
